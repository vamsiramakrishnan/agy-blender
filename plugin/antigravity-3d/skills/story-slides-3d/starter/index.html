How heavy is a cloud?

A short talk with one cloud in it.

Let the cloud drift for a moment before you speak. Ask the room for a guess, in kilograms, and take two or three.

What it looks like

It looks like nothing at all.

From underneath it is a shadow going over a house. Nobody ducks.

Take an ordinary one: a kilometre long, wide and tall.

A small fair-weather cumulus. Point at the house: it is still there, at the edge of the shadow.

What it is made of

It is water. Drops a fiftieth of a millimetre across.

Not vapour: vapour is invisible. What you see is liquid water, in drops too small to fall like rain.

Half a gram of them in every cubic metre.

All the drops in a cubic metre, gathered up, make one bead the size of a pea. That is the whole premise: say it slowly.

A hundred thousand million million drops in all.

Each dot stands for some forty million million drops. There are a thousand million of those cubic metres.

What it weighs

Pour them together.

Say nothing while it pours. The number is better if they have built the cube themselves first.

A cube of water eight metres on a side.

Taller than the house. A thousand million cubic metres of cloud come to this.

500 tonnes

1 km3 x 0.5 g per m3

About a hundred elephants, or a loaded jumbo jet and a half. Compare it with the guesses from the first slide.

Why it stays up

So why is it up there?

Let the question stand while the water goes back up.

Each drop falls about a centimetre a second.

The marks on the ruler are a centimetre apart. Count one of them aloud: that is as fast as it gets.

The air under the cloud rises a hundred times faster.

A metre a second, on a warm day. The drops are falling the whole time, through air that is going up.

Look up.

Five hundred tonnes, held up by a breeze.

Stop here. If there is a window, point at it.
